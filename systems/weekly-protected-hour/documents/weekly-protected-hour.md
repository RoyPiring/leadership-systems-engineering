## Choosing the Work That Deserves a Weekly Appointment

### Defining the delayed item and its first visible piece

My delayed item was updating my resume. It mattered to me, but it had remained unscheduled and was repeatedly left for spare time. I reduced that broad item to one visible piece that could be completed in a single sitting: rewriting the resume summary paragraph and producing at least its first new sentence.

Defining the smallest piece gave the weekly appointment a concrete starting point. I would not need to decide whether to revise the entire resume when the reminder appeared. I could open the file, find the summary, and begin one named change. This reduced the distance between seeing the cue and creating visible evidence that the work had started.

## Creating a Home for the Practice

### Setting up the project workspace

I created a one-page Notion workspace for the four-week baseline, scheduled practice, first-session plan, movement rule, boundary statement, cost note, and later review. The page gave the habit one continuing record rather than spreading its rules and results across memory, email, and separate notes.

I used a conservative baseline to test whether the resume was genuinely neglected before giving it a recurring appointment. The page would record each of the four past weeks as Yes or No based on visible calendar evidence. This established the starting condition before I added the weekly cue, preventing later activity from being confused with what had happened before the practice began.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/e5ae7613-e3ee-4542-b52b-8550151a5589_rxciijwu)

### Confirming the shared Google Account

I opened Google Calendar and Gmail in separate browser tabs and checked the profile button in the upper-right corner of each tool. Both showed Roy Piring Jr and the same personal Gmail address. This confirmed that the calendar events and email reminder copies would remain connected to one account.

The check protected the practice from an avoidable setup error. A calendar cue created under one account and an expected email notification checked under another could make a valid reminder appear missing. Confirming the shared identity first meant later notifications, review events, and reminder emails could be traced to the same account without switching profiles or searching another inbox.

## Establishing an Honest Baseline

### Recording four weeks of evidence

I reviewed the four weeks before the first scheduled session and recorded whether each week contained visible calendar evidence of resume work. The baseline used only what the calendar could support. It did not treat intention, memory, or an unrecorded plan as proof that the work had happened.

This created a defensible starting point for the weekly practice. Each week received a Yes only if the calendar showed related time and a No when it did not. Recording the baseline before creating the recurring event kept earlier neglect separate from future sessions. It also gave the later review a clear account of why the resume qualified for a protected weekly appointment.

### Using calendar silence as conservative evidence

I searched all four prior weeks for calendar entries related to my resume and found none. Because the calendar could not show that the work occurred, I recorded No for each week and noted that the calendar was silent. I did not replace missing evidence with a guess based on what I may have intended or remembered.

This rule made the baseline intentionally conservative. An undocumented work session may have happened, but the selected evidence source could not prove it. Counting a silent week as Yes would have made the neglect appear smaller without support. The four No entries therefore represented what the calendar established, not a claim that no resume thought or activity of any kind occurred.

## Turning a Calendar Event into a Weekly Cue

### Building a recurring cue for the real work

I turned the resume update into a recurring appointment called Update my resume every Sunday from 3:00 to 4:00 PM. The event included a pop-up reminder, an email reminder, the Notion page link, and the Session 1 instruction. This gave the work its own hour instead of requiring it to compete for leftover time.

The event description also named what I would do during the first five minutes. Writing that response in advance removed another decision from the start of the session. When the reminder appeared, I would already know which page to open, which file to use, and which paragraph to change. The cue therefore connected a specific time with a prepared first action.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/e5ae7613-e3ee-4542-b52b-8550151a5589_cy3ckxyf)

### Committing to a first-five-minute response

When the Sunday reminder appeared, I would open the Notion page, read the Session 1 line, and open the resume file. I would then begin rewriting the summary paragraph and type its first new sentence before five minutes had passed. The response focused on starting, not completing the entire resume.

The first visible sentence served as evidence that I had crossed from preparation into action. It also reduced the chance that I would open email, reconsider the plan, or postpone the session while deciding where to begin. The appointment protected the hour, while the first-five-minute rule protected the start of that hour from delay and negotiation.

## Protecting the Hour Before Pressure Arrives

### Preparing the boundary, movement rule, and cost note

I prepared for requests that could compete with the Sunday appointment before anyone made one. The page included a boundary sentence: “I have something at 3:00 PM on Sunday, but I’m free any time after 4:00 PM.” This protected the hour without requiring an apology or a detailed explanation of the work.

I also wrote the same-week movement result and added a blank cost line. If the session had to move, the rule would keep it inside the same week rather than allowing it to disappear. The cost line would remain blank until after the fourth session, preventing an early judgment about what protecting the appointment required. Preparing these elements in advance reduced the need to invent rules under pressure.

## Scheduling a Fair 66-Day Review

### Setting a separate review reminder

I scheduled the review before any weekly results existed. The first session was Sunday, October 11, and the one-time review was set for Wednesday evening, December 16, 66 days later. The review event included both a pop-up and an email notification so the later decision had its own cue.

Separating the review from the weekly appointments prevented routine session reminders from becoming informal judgment points. December 16 was reserved for counting the defined review weeks and applying the written rule. Scheduling it at the start also reduced the chance that I would delay evaluation, choose a more favorable date, or assess the habit only when I felt satisfied with the outcome.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/e5ae7613-e3ee-4542-b52b-8550151a5589_xo7w6oud)

### Freezing the Keep and Drop outcomes

I wrote the Keep and Drop outcomes before counting a single review week. A total of 3 or 4 meant I would keep the Sunday appointment. A total of 2 or fewer meant I would drop it. The page also stated that the cutoff would not move after the results became known.

This rule made the December 16 decision mechanical rather than persuasive. I would read the recorded total and follow the matching outcome instead of explaining away missed sessions or lowering the boundary to preserve a habit I wanted to keep. Freezing the interpretation first separated the evidence from my later preference and gave both possible outcomes a declared meaning.

## Applying the Rule Without Changing the Measure

### Using the frozen cutoff to decide the appointment's future

The future decision would use the same review measure and cutoff written before the weekly sessions began. On December 16, I would count the defined review weeks and compare the total with the fixed boundary. A result of 3 or 4 would retain the Sunday appointment, while 2 or fewer would remove it.

The review had not yet occurred, so the appointment’s future remained undecided. The value of the setup was that neither a good nor disappointing result could change how the total would be interpreted. Notion held the rule, Calendar held the cue and review date, and Gmail received the reminder copies. Together, those records made the later decision traceable without adding a new measure after seeing the outcome.

## Reflecting on the Practice and Its Tools

### Tools and concepts applied

I used Notion for the one-page record, Google Calendar for the recurring Sunday cue and one-time 66-day review, and Gmail for the email copies of each reminder. The system connected the written plan, scheduled action, and delayed decision across one confirmed Google Account.

I learned to treat calendar silence as No, establish a four-week conservative baseline, protect one recurring hour, and prewrite the first-five-minute response. I also fixed the 3-out-of-4 cutoff before counting and defined how a moved session would remain inside the same week. The central lesson was to decide the rules before results could influence them.

### Time invested and challenges encountered

The setup took a little more than one hour across one evening. Most of that time went into reading the previous four calendar weeks, recording the baseline, and configuring the weekly event’s description, link, pop-up, and email reminder. The 66-day review remained ahead, so the full practice would continue through mid-December.

The main challenge was turning a broad intention to update my resume into a scheduled action with a clear start. The calendar event alone was not enough. It also needed a protected boundary, movement rule, first-session instruction, and fixed review outcome. Each element reduced a different opportunity to postpone the work once the Sunday cue appeared.

### Next learning goal

This practice became more concrete than a general promise to work on my resume. The update now had a real Sunday appointment from 3:00 to 4:00 PM, a prepared response for the first five minutes, and a later review whose outcomes were written before the first session.

My next goal is to observe whether the first-five-minute rule gets me into the resume when the reminder appears. I want to see whether opening the page, reading the Session 1 line, opening the file, and typing one new sentence is enough to carry me into the protected hour. The December review will then determine whether the recurring appointment remains.
