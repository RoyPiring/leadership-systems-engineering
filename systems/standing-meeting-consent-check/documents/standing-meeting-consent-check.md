## Choosing a Respectful Meeting Check

### Selecting an eligible update meeting

I designed a private before-and-after check for one Monday morning team update that I run in person. The meeting usually lasts about fifteen minutes and focuses on short updates, making it narrow enough for four baseline observations followed by four meetings using the proposed standing format.

Participation remained voluntary throughout the check. Chairs would stay available, anyone could sit without explanation, and any refusal would stop the standing phase before it began. The Notion page recorded only timing and whether content was reopened or missed. It did not collect names, health information, refusal reasons, or claims about why the meeting changed.

## Creating a Sitting-First Meeting Plan

### Building the meeting page

I created a private Notion page with a narrow title, a plain summary, and THE PRACTICE. Before adding the content, I confirmed that Notion, Gmail, and Google Calendar opened under my own accounts. The page remained in Notion’s Private section rather than becoming a shared team record.

The summary placed permission to sit before any mention of standing. This established the tone of the check before timing, consent, or format details appeared. Gmail would later carry the note after Meeting 4, while Calendar held only the Day 66 review. The page remained the main location for definitions, sequence, logs, limits, and the decision rule.

### Confirming the fixed meeting order

The written sequence contained four meetings in their existing format, followed by the consent note, followed by four meetings offered in the standing format. The page stated near the title that anyone who preferred to sit could sit and that chairs would remain available throughout all eight observations.

Fixing this order before recording times prevented me from selecting favorable meetings or moving the standing phase after seeing early results. Meetings 1 through 4 established the baseline, and Meetings 5 through 8 supplied the comparison only after the note was sent. The sequence therefore defined when the format could change and kept voluntary sitting available during both phases.

## Defining Clear Boundaries and Measures

### Setting consistent counting rules

I wrote the eligibility and measurement rules before timing a meeting. Under THIS MEETING IS, I checked the Monday update against all nine conditions. I then defined TIME, REOPENED OR MISSED, DROP, the KEEP-OR-STOP SENTENCE, and the structure of each LOG entry.

These definitions gave every meeting the same counting method. Two quiet clock readings would establish the start and finish, while reopened or missed content would be recorded under the same written rule. Defining DROP before observing results also fixed the one result number. A later reader could follow the page and classify the same meeting without depending on an explanation invented after the eight observations.

## Protecting Consent Before Recording Results

### Writing the consent note and decision rule

I drafted the note that would be sent only after Meeting 4. It began by stating that anyone who preferred to sit should sit, then asked the team to try the next four Monday updates standing by the whiteboard. The note also allowed anyone to decline privately without providing a reason.

I wrote the keep-or-stop rule before collecting any clock readings. This kept a favorable timing result from overriding a refusal or changing the original decision boundary. The note would remain on the Notion page until the four baseline meetings were complete, preventing the standing request from influencing the before phase or being sent earlier than the written sequence allowed.

### Keeping refusals private

If anyone declined, the only retained statement would be No. Stopped before the standing meetings. I would not record the person’s name, ask why, or describe the circumstances. The refusal itself would be enough to end the proposed standing phase before Meetings 5 through 8 began.

This rule made saying No simple and private. It prevented the page from becoming a record of personal needs or reasons and avoided placing anyone in a position where they had to defend a preference to sit. The meeting check depended on consent, so stopping after a refusal was a successful use of the safety rule rather than a failed attempt to collect timing data.

## Locking the Sequence and Recording Change

### Creating the before-and-after log

I added the BEFORE AND AFTER sequence and a completed example log before recording any real meeting. The sequence showed Meetings 1 through 4, the line Then send the note., and Meetings 5 through 8. Each real row would later need only the two quiet clock readings and the defined observation fields.

I checked the example calculation by subtracting 50 from 60, producing a DROP of 10. This confirmed how the single result number would be derived without claiming that the real meetings would produce the same outcome. The example tested the layout and arithmetic, while the empty real rows preserved the fact that no measured result existed yet.

### Fixing the order before outcomes are known

The page placed Meetings 1 through 4 in the existing format before the consent note and Meetings 5 through 8 in the proposed standing format. I verified that Then send the note. appeared exactly between the two phases. Permission to sit remained visible throughout the second phase.

Writing the sequence before timing prevented phase swapping, selective meeting choice, or an early format change based on promising results. The baseline had to finish before the request could be sent, and the comparison could proceed only if no one declined. This made the order, consent point, and sitting option part of the method rather than adjustments introduced during execution.

### Applying limits and cautious interpretation

The limits stated that standing would not fix a long meeting or a crowded agenda. The research note also distinguished the cited study from this check: the study concerned groups making decisions, while the selected meeting involved updates. It ended by stating that the research established nothing certain about my Monday meeting.

These boundaries limited the conclusion to the eight observations recorded on the page. Even if DROP showed a shorter standing phase, the log could not establish that standing caused the change. Differences in agenda, attendance, discussion, or other conditions could still matter. The result would remain a description of one recurring update rather than advice for meetings in general.

## Auditing the Page Without Making Claims

### Checking order and sitting-first language

I reread the summary, BEFORE AND AFTER sequence, and example log. Each showed four meetings in the existing format, then the note, then four meetings in the proposed standing format. I also verified that the summary, THE PRACTICE, and consent note began with Anyone who would rather sit, please sit.

I counted the characters in each required line and confirmed that every line remained under 60 characters. I then repeated the example arithmetic and confirmed that 60 minus 50 still produced a DROP of 10. This audit checked structure, consent language, line length, and calculation without adding a real outcome or changing the planned sequence.

### Avoiding outcome and cause claims

I reviewed every sentence that discussed results and confirmed that none predicted a shorter meeting or claimed that standing caused a change. The page retained the statements This page records how this meeting changed. and It cannot tell me why it changed. These sentences separated observation from explanation.

The three limits and research note also remained intact. I added no recommendation for other meetings and made no claim that the selected format would work for another team, agenda, or setting. The page therefore stayed a private record for one Monday update. Its future timing result could describe a difference, but it could not establish cause or support a broad meeting practice.

## Reflecting on the Meeting Check

### Tools and concepts applied

I used Notion for the private plan, consent language, rules, example, logs, limits, and final review record. Google Calendar held the 8 December review event, while Gmail would carry the consent note only after Meeting 4. No standing request was sent during setup.

The main concepts were sitting-first language, private refusal, fixed before-and-after order, consistent timing definitions, and one DROP result. I also wrote the keep-or-stop rule before any observation and limited the conclusion to one meeting. The central lesson was that a log can show how a meeting changed without proving why it changed or overriding a person’s choice to sit.

### Project timing and challenges

The setup required approximately one and a half hours at my desk. The live portion would add two quiet clock readings at each of the next eight Monday meetings. The Day 66 review on 8 December would return me to the prewritten decision rule after the observations were complete.

The most demanding part was the page audit. Counting characters line by line, checking sitting-first language in three locations, confirming the phase order, and repeating the example calculation took longer than expected. That work mattered because a small wording or sequence change could weaken consent, alter the method, or introduce a claim the evidence could not support.

### Continuing the learning journey

I completed this work to test a small meeting-format change without pressuring anyone or overstating the result. Writing the refusal rule and keep-or-stop decision before timing made the method feel fair. The page also kept permission to sit visible before, during, and after the proposed standing phase.

My next goal is to learn how to run a short team retrospective focused on one decision while making room for every voice. I want to carry forward the same controls: define the question first, make opting out safe, record only what is needed, and avoid turning a local observation into a claim that reaches beyond the evidence.
