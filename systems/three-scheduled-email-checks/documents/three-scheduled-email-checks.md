## Setting a Safe Boundary for Email Checks

### Protecting urgent communication channels

I tested whether checking Gmail at three scheduled times would reduce how often I opened it compared with my normal behavior. I used one Notion page for the safety boundary, definitions, tally marks, break list, costs, and verdicts. Google Calendar held the Day 8 switch, Day 14 reading, and Day 66 verdict reminders.

The experiment applied only to email that could wait. My on-call pager, phone calls, text messages, and security alerts remained active throughout the 66 days. I wrote the rules and both verdict branches before collecting results so the observed counts could not change the cutoff. This made reduced Gmail checking subordinate to the safety boundary rather than treating fewer checks as the only goal.

## Designing the Rules Before the Results

### Creating the page, rules, and reminders

I created one Notion page containing the safety boundary, check definition, Week 1 and Week 2 tallies, fixed check times, break list, cost area, and Day 66 verdict branches. I selected 8:30 AM, 12:30 PM, and 4:30 PM as the three Gmail times that would begin on Day 8.

I also created only three Google Calendar events: Day 8: Switch, Day 14: Read, and Day 66: Verdict. I did not create daily calendar events for the check times during the baseline. Recording the full method before Week 1 established what would change, what would remain protected, how checks would be counted, and how the later result would be judged.

### Precommitting to safety checks and verdicts

I recorded my concerns and decision rules before the first tally could influence them. I predicted that a teammate might ask why I was slow, and I defined the SERIOUS condition as missing an urgent work request. I also copied the KEEP and DROP branches onto the page exactly as they would be applied on Day 66.

This order kept later interpretation from being shaped around the result I preferred. If I had written the safety concern, cutoff, or verdict language after counting, I could have minimized a problem or changed the rule to preserve the routine. Precommitting meant the experiment had to satisfy the original safety and reduction conditions rather than a standard created after the evidence was known.

## Capturing an Unchanged Email-Checking Baseline

### Recording normal checking habits

I recorded every Gmail check from Monday the 12th through Sunday the 18th. Each time I opened Gmail, I added one | mark to the matching day while the action was still fresh. I did not reconstruct the week afterward from browser history, memory, or an estimate of how busy the day had felt.

The tallies showed that checking varied across the week, with a busy weekday such as Thursday reaching 12 marks. This baseline supplied the reference for Week 2. Recording each event when it happened protected the comparison from recall errors and made frequent, automatic checks visible. The goal was to capture normal behavior honestly before introducing the three-time routine.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/85c8487b-3337-4c72-b0e9-aeebd017090f_fqizun1g)

### Preserving a fair Week 1 comparison

I kept my usual Gmail behavior throughout the baseline and changed only the tallying. Gmail notifications stayed at their existing setting, the STAYS ON channels remained active, and the three written check times did not yet control when I opened the inbox. I added each mark at the moment of the check.

I also left the Week 2 tally, DROP result, and COST note blank until their assigned stages. This prevented future observations from entering the baseline record and kept the two weeks distinct. Week 1 therefore represented my existing checking pattern, while Week 2 could measure the coordinated routine against evidence collected under unchanged conditions.

## Switching to Three Intentional Check Times

### Starting the coordinated Day 8 switch

On Day 8, I started the full routine at one defined boundary. Eligible alerts were off, Gmail remained closed between scheduled checks, and the three daily check times became active. The saved settings path confirmed that desktop notifications were already set to Mail notifications off, so no additional Gmail notification change was required.

I closed the Gmail tab, opened it at 8:30 AM, and added the first Week 2 tally mark. Beginning every treatment element together gave Week 2 one clear starting point. It also limited the conclusion: the result could describe the coordinated routine, but it could not determine whether the schedule, closed tab, notification state, or combination of those elements caused the change.

### Separating eligible alerts from protected channels

I defined an alert path as eligible only when it carried email that could safely wait until the next scheduled check. The only eligible path was Gmail desktop notifications in Chrome on my laptop. That path did not carry my on-call pager, phone calls, text messages, or security alerts.

Those urgent channels remained under STAYS ON and outside the experiment. This separation prevented a reduction in Gmail interruptions from hiding time-sensitive communication. It also gave the SERIOUS condition a clear safety purpose: if the routine caused me to miss an urgent work request, that result would matter regardless of how much the email tally fell.

## Tracking a Week of Deliberate Email Checks

### Marking every Week 2 check

From Days 8 through 14, I followed the three-time Gmail routine and marked every check when it occurred. Most days ended with three marks, matching the planned 8:30 AM, 12:30 PM, and 4:30 PM checks. I also counted any opening outside those times instead of hiding it as an exception.

Monday and Wednesday each received a fourth mark because I checked between the scheduled windows. Including those extra looks kept the Week 2 total comparable with the baseline rule, where every check counted. The SERIOUS condition never occurred during the week. This record showed both adherence and breaks without rewriting the routine as more consistent than it had been.

## Measuring the Change and Recording Its Costs

### Reading the break list, DROP, and costs

On Day 14, I completed the break list before counting either week. The predicted vendor delay occurred, the predicted teammate question did not occur, and the SERIOUS condition remained No. Recording these outcomes first prevented the final reduction number from influencing how I remembered the costs or safety events.

I then counted Week 1 and Week 2 once and recorded a single DROP value of 37. I described the cost in words rather than creating another score that could compete with the main measure. This order preserved one quantitative outcome for the checking change while keeping side effects and concerns visible as qualitative evidence.

## Applying the Prewritten Day 66 Verdict

### Using the original KEEP or DROP rule

On Day 66, I returned to the original Week 1 and Week 2 marks and applied the KEEP and DROP branches written on Day 1. Week 2 was well under half of Week 1, and the SERIOUS line still read No. Those conditions matched the original KEEP branch, so I wrote KEEP under DAY 66 VERDICT.

I did not add another metric, recalculate the test with a different formula, or create a new cutoff based on how the routine felt that day. The verdict came directly from the prewritten rule and retained evidence. This made the final decision traceable to the original boundary rather than to memory, preference, or a desire to preserve the habit after investing 66 days in it.

## Reflecting on the Email-Checking Practice

### Tools and concepts applied

I used Notion to store the safety boundary, definitions, tally marks, break list, costs, DROP value, and final verdict. Google Calendar held the three dated checkpoints, while Gmail supplied the behavior being measured and the desktop notification setting used during the routine.

The main concepts were protecting urgent channels, recording each check at the moment it happened, keeping the baseline unchanged, switching all treatment elements on one date, and defining safety events before results existed. I also learned to use one DROP number and preserve costs in words. The strongest control was writing both verdict branches first so the later outcome could not negotiate its own rule.

### Time invested and key challenge

The work required about one hour of hands-on time spread across 66 days. Creating the Notion page and three Calendar checkpoints took approximately 40 minutes. Each tally mark required only a few seconds during the two measured weeks, the Day 14 reading took about 10 minutes, and the Day 66 verdict took about 5 minutes.

The main challenge was counting unscheduled checks honestly. Monday and Wednesday each included a fourth look between the planned times, and those checks still had to enter the record. Omitting them would have made the routine appear cleaner and the comparison less credible. The prewritten safety and verdict rules made the final review faster because no new decision method was needed.

### Next learning goal

This practice made me notice how often I opened Gmail without a deliberate reason. The three scheduled windows created a visible alternative to checking whenever the thought appeared, while the unchanged urgent channels kept the experiment inside its safety boundary. Writing the KEEP and DROP outcomes before collecting numbers made the final verdict feel fair.

My next step is to add a clear Day 14 Yes or No field beside every break-list prediction. That would make the vendor delay, teammate question, SERIOUS condition, and future predictions easier to review in one place. I would keep those fields qualitative so they document whether each expected event occurred without creating a second score beside the DROP measure.
