## Committing to a Personal Planning Review

### The planning habit under review

I reviewed every planning habit I currently used or had already stopped. Before opening any supporting record, I wrote a Rule for each habit that identified its purpose, one Number to inspect, a measurement window, and the condition that would lead to DROP. I then planned to assign each habit one verdict: DROP, HOLD, or KEEP.

I kept the inventory on one Notion Practice Page and scheduled a dated review for December 8, 2026. This mattered because familiar routines can continue without proof that they still serve their purpose. Writing the tests before checking the evidence reduced my ability to protect a favored habit by lowering its standard after seeing an inconvenient result.

## Creating a Complete Planning Practice Inventory

### Setting up the Practice Page

I created a Notion page named Practice Page and opened Google Calendar and Gmail in separate browser tabs. Under JUDGING, Sat 3 Oct 2026, I listed four planning habits, including habits still in use and inbox skimming, which I had already stopped. Recording the complete inventory first ensured that discontinued or uncomfortable practices remained part of the review.

The page became the single decision record for the rules, numbers, verdicts, routine, and future review. Google Calendar and Gmail remained evidence sources rather than alternate copies of the decision page. Listing all four habits before writing their results also prevented me from excluding one simply because its records were incomplete or its outcome was likely to be unfavorable.

### Confirming Calendar and Gmail access

Google Calendar and Gmail opened under the same Google Account without requesting another sign-in. Calendar loaded in week view, and Gmail opened to the inbox in the neighboring tab. This confirmed that the two primary evidence sources were available before I began counting any dated events or reviewing email-related records.

The access check did not produce a result for any planning habit. It only verified that I could reach the sources named in the later rules. Keeping this step separate from measurement preserved the intended order: prepare the inventory, confirm the sources, write the decision rules, and only then inspect the evidence needed to calculate each Number.

## Setting a Fair Future Review Process

### Fixing the review routine before checking Numbers

I wrote one review sentence beginning with On Tuesday, 8 December 2026 before inspecting any Number. The sentence covered the Rule, Number, Verdict, conditions for HOLD, conditions for KEEP only, stopping dropped habits, and starting one new Practice. I read it aloud to confirm that every required condition appeared in the decision sequence.

Fixing the routine in advance kept the December review from becoming an open-ended reconsideration. The same sentence would guide the later decision regardless of whether the records looked encouraging or disappointing. It also prevented today’s verdicts from becoming permanent because every surviving practice would return to a dated review instead of continuing indefinitely through familiarity.

## Defining Rules Before Reviewing Evidence

### Writing fixed Rules before opening records

With Gmail, Google Calendar, and my older pages closed, I wrote a fixed Rule for each of the four practices. I crossed out KEEP if I used it 7 times. because that statement measured frequency without checking the habit’s purpose. I replaced it with a For: line and a DROP if: line containing one Number and a defined time window.

I also marked inbox skimming as Stopped before reviewing its record. One example rule required at least 3 Sunday look-backs within the previous 4 weeks. Writing each threshold with the source closed established the standard without knowing the answer. The rules could later be judged as useful or poorly chosen, but they could not be rewritten to protect a preferred habit during this review.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/builtwithroy/uploads/d7cc6b91-c570-4471-a0c1-97bcf790e242_nx2li3fy)

### Why Rules came before records

If I saw a Number first, I could place the cutoff just below it and make a familiar habit pass. To prevent that, I completed all four For: and DROP if: lines before opening Calendar, Gmail, or an older practice page. The rule for Sunday look-backs, for example, required at least 3 completed reviews within a fixed 4-week window.

This sequence separated evaluation design from evaluation results. Each verdict would compare an observed Number with a threshold selected while the outcome was unknown. Prewriting the rule did not guarantee that the threshold was ideal, but it made the process inspectable and reduced the influence of hindsight. Any future threshold change would belong to a new review period rather than altering the current result.

## Using Evidence to Judge Planning Practices

### Checking Numbers and assigning Verdicts

After all rules were fixed, I opened the supporting records and copied the relevant Number beneath each habit. Sunday look-back received DROP with 2 held. Email routing received HOLD with 3 of 20 items put off. The list rebuild received DROP because its record contained no completed Number. Inbox skimming received DROP because it was already marked Stopped.

I did not change a purpose, threshold, time window, or verdict order after opening the evidence. This preserved the connection between each prewritten test and its outcome. HOLD remained available only where a usable Number existed but the evidence did not support KEEP. A missing Number did not become zero or an estimate; it remained missing and led to DROP under the fixed process.

### Matching each Number to its Rule

For each habit, I counted only the outcome identified in its For: line and only within the period defined by its DROP if: condition. For Sunday look-back, I counted review events recorded in Google Calendar during the 4 weeks before October 3. For email routing, I counted put-off entries in the routing page’s running log.

The list rebuild page did not contain a completed count, so I omitted its Number line rather than guessing or reconstructing one from incomplete notes. This preserved the distinction between no recorded evidence and a recorded result of zero. Matching every Number to one source, outcome, and time window made the verdicts repeatable and kept unrelated activity from entering the calculation.

## Turning Verdicts into a Routine and Review Date

### Creating the Routine and Calendar review

I converted the verdicts into a routine I could follow until the next review. Under ROUTINE, I entered the only surviving habit: routing each email immediately after reading it. I placed RESULT above the routine and created a Google Calendar event titled Judge my planning habits for December 8, 2026.

The event included one notification and the four review lines needed to repeat the decision. This meant today’s HOLD would not silently become permanent. Email routing could continue as a temporary preference while collecting a new Number, but it would need to face the same written review process in December. All dropped habits remained outside the active routine.

### Calculating the Kept count Result

I counted the KEEP verdicts under JUDGING and compared them with the total number of listed practices. None of the four habits received KEEP, so I entered Kept count: 0, of 4 listed. The denominator included inbox skimming even though it was already stopped because it appeared in the original inventory and still required a verdict.

The result was humbling but consistent with the recorded decisions. The email-routing habit did not increase the Kept count because its verdict was HOLD, not KEEP. It remained in the routine only until the future review produced enough evidence for a final decision. Keeping HOLD separate prevented a temporary practice from being reported as one that had already passed its standard.

## Making the Next Review Reproducible

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/builtwithroy/uploads/d7cc6b91-c570-4471-a0c1-97bcf790e242_me1f7xj5)

### Using record locations to repeat the process

Each Next record: line identified the exact location of the future Number and the amount of history to count. For email routing, the line pointed to the MY RUNNING LOG section of the routing page. Its measurement window matched the one defined in the corresponding DROP if: rule, so the evidence source and decision condition remained aligned.

On December 8, I can move directly from the rule to the named record, count the put-off lines inside the fixed window, and assign a verdict without searching through unrelated pages. This makes the next review easier to repeat and reduces the risk of selecting a more favorable source later. The record location does not decide the verdict, but it makes the evidence path visible.

## Reflecting on Planning Tools and Progress

### Tools and decision-making concepts learned

I used Notion as the single Practice Page, Google Calendar for the dated review and Sunday-look-back evidence, Gmail for inbox-related records, and older pages for prior habit results. Each tool served a defined role. The Practice Page stored decisions, while the other sources supplied Numbers tied to the rules written before inspection.

The main concepts were prewriting one Rule per habit, checking one Number within a fixed window, applying verdicts in a set order, and allowing HOLD only when evidence existed. I also described the resulting routine as a personal preference rather than proof. A small record can guide my next action, but it cannot show that a habit caused a broader change in planning performance.

### Time invested and key challenge

The review took approximately 1 hour and 10 minutes from setup through the final calendar event. Most of that time went into writing honest rules for four different habits and then locating the matching records. The page’s estimate of 55 minutes would have been reasonable if the records had been cleaner and each Number had been stored in an obvious location.

The hardest part was separating a habit’s purpose from simple usage. A routine can be performed frequently without achieving what it was intended to do. Crossing out the seven-use test forced me to define what each practice was for and what result would make it fail. Careful counting also prevented incomplete records from being treated as stronger evidence than they were.

### Next learning goal

This review forced me to examine planning habits that had continued without a recent decision. I kept the Practice Page as the permanent record and left the December 8 calendar notification active. The current routine contains only email routing, and that habit remains on HOLD rather than receiving an early KEEP verdict.

My next goal is to determine whether email routing earns KEEP when I repeat the process in December. The Next record: line already identifies the running log and measurement window, so the next decision should require less searching. I will apply the written rule to the new Number and avoid changing the threshold after seeing the result.
