## Setting an Intentional Look-Back Practice

### Preparing an honest record of dated plans

I prepared the workspace before counting anything from the previous seven days. I confirmed that Notepad and Chrome opened correctly, checked that Google Calendar loaded, and created practice-page.txt. The file began with one sentence explaining why I wanted a record of what happened to plans that had specific dates. This gave the review one stable home before any result existed.

Starting with the purpose protected the record from becoming a personal scorecard. The page would describe completed and slipped plans without treating either result as a judgment about me. Saving and reopening the file confirmed that the first sentence remained in place. That simple check also made sure I was working from a saved record rather than temporary text that could disappear before the review was finished.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/builtwithroy/uploads/17ec13ec-eed0-4f2c-a0f3-5e45b3bf9344_ikflxrmj)

### Naming the Practice Page and its purpose

The first line stated that practice-page.txt was my Practice Page and that I wanted an honest record of what happened to plans I had given a date. I saved the file, closed it, and opened it again to verify that the sentence persisted. Naming the file and its purpose before adding the layout made the reason for every later count visible at the top.

The sentence also stated that plans I allowed to slide still belonged in the record, including the uncomfortable ones. That boundary reduced the temptation to remember only completed work or exclude plans that made the week look crowded. The page was not designed to reward a low count. It was designed to preserve a useful account of dated commitments, their outcomes, and the decisions I made after a slip.

## Building the Four-Part Look-Back System

### Creating one home for every review cadence

I added one LOOK-BACK section beneath the opening sentence and divided it into WEEKLY, EVENING, MONTHLY, and EVERY THREE MONTHS. The weekly area included blank fields for the Slipped count, total dated items, and how the week felt. The other areas created locations for shorter daily reflections and less frequent reviews without requiring separate files or tracking systems.

I left all weekly result fields empty during setup because the count had not begun. This preserved the difference between preparing the structure and entering observations. Each review cadence had a known location, but none contained an invented number or early conclusion. Keeping everything in one plain-text file also made the record easy to reopen without depending on a database, template, or specialized tracking app.

### Putting the Slipped Count in context

The weekly line used the format Slipped count: __, of __ dated items so the number of slips would always appear beside the total number of dated commitments. A count without its denominator could make two difficult weeks appear identical even when one contained far more planned work. The total supplied basic workload context without turning the record into a percentage or rating.

For example, two slips among four dated items describe a different week from two slips among fifteen. Neither number explains why the slips happened, but the denominator prevents the numerator from standing alone. I also kept the week-felt line in words so I could describe the week without creating another score. The result remained descriptive: what slipped, how much was dated, and how the week felt.

## Defining a Personal Keep-or-Stop Review

### Creating a future decision point for the practice

I created a KEEP OR STOP section and fixed the review date as December 8, 2026. I rewrote each decision condition in my own words, using one line for every part of the rule. This ensured that I understood what would support keeping the practice and what would support stopping it, rather than copying language that I might interpret differently after seeing several weeks of results.

Setting the decision before the first count prevented one difficult week from ending the practice early. It also prevented routine from keeping the practice alive after it stopped being useful. The December review would use the conditions already written on the page. I could still describe costs and experience honestly, but I could not rewrite the decision test simply because the accumulated counts made one outcome feel more comfortable.

## Measuring Slips with Context and Care

### Counting dated commitments from memory

I reviewed the previous seven days using only what I remembered that day. I listed 9 dated items and identified 5 slip occurrences. The photo backup appeared twice because its first replacement date also passed without completion. I gave every slip one next decision: assign a new date, drop the item, or hand it to someone else. No slipped item remained in the record without a stated disposition.

I also wrote that the week felt busy. This added personal context without changing the count or excusing any item. Because the review was memory-only, it was an honest reconstruction rather than a complete audit of every calendar entry or message. The record described what I could recall at that review point and preserved the limitation instead of presenting the 5 slips as a perfect historical total.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/builtwithroy/uploads/17ec13ec-eed0-4f2c-a0f3-5e45b3bf9344_uffnb43x)

### Using dated items to interpret the count

The completed line read Slipped count: 5, of 9 dated items, followed by the note that the week felt busy. Reading 5 beside 9 showed that the slips occurred within a week containing nine dated commitments. The total did not make the missed dates acceptable, but it prevented the count from being interpreted without any indication of how much dated work I had attempted.

I treated the result as a description of that week rather than a grade on my ability or character. The numbers did not explain workload, difficulty, interruptions, or whether the plans were realistic. They showed only the relationship between remembered slips and remembered dated items. The written week-felt line supplied limited context while keeping the actual count unchanged and visible.

## Recording What Went Well

### Capturing three good things and why they happened

I created an EVENING entry dated October 17, 2026, and recorded three distinct things that went well. Under each item, I added a Why line describing what supported it. One example was blocking an hour early, which connected the positive outcome to an action I could recognize and potentially repeat rather than leaving the entry as a general statement that the day went well.

I marked MONTHLY and EVERY THREE MONTHS as not yet because those review points had not arrived. I did not fill them early or infer what their results would be. The evening entry contained no rating, score, or comparison with another day. Its purpose was to balance attention to slips with evidence of useful actions and conditions, not to cancel out the weekly count or manufacture a positive result.

## Creating Reliable Review Cues

### Scheduling each review cadence

I created five Google Calendar cues. Weekly Look-Back repeated every Sunday at 6:00 PM, and Evening Three repeated daily at 9:00 PM. I also created one-time events for November 3, December 8, and January 3. Two cues repeated automatically, while the remaining three marked specific review dates connected to the longer practice schedule.

Each event description contained either the slip-count rules or the exact review question required on that date. This reduced the need to remember the procedure when a notification appeared. The calendar supplied the cue, while practice-page.txt remained the only home for the actual record. Separating reminders from results avoided creating two competing versions of the same practice.

## Tracking Repeat Slips Across Review Windows

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/builtwithroy/uploads/17ec13ec-eed0-4f2c-a0f3-5e45b3bf9344_hvhxbluu)

### Recognizing when a replacement date creates a new slip

The photo backup first slipped and received a replacement date of Tuesday, October 6. That second date also passed while the task remained unfinished, creating another slip occurrence. I added the repeat-slip rule and recorded both weekly entries beneath the count. Each entry showed Slips in this entry: 1 and included its own next decision.

This method counted missed dates rather than unique tasks. One item could therefore create more than one slip when it received another specific date and missed that date as well. Recording occurrences exposed how repeatedly rescheduling unfinished work could increase the count over time. It also kept each missed commitment connected to the decision made at that point instead of collapsing the two failures into one vague overdue item.

## Reflecting on the Practice Built

### Tools and concepts explored

I used Notepad for one plain-text Practice Page and Google Calendar in Chrome for five return cues. The file held the weekly, evening, monthly, and three-month review areas, while Calendar brought me back on the scheduled dates. This kept the written evidence separate from the notification system and prevented the reminders from becoming a second record.

The main concepts were a memory-only Slipped Count, the total number of dated items for context, one decision for every slip, and three evening successes with one reason each. I also fixed the December keep-or-stop conditions before collecting the first result. Together, these boundaries kept the practice focused on observation and response instead of self-scoring or changing the rule after seeing the numbers.

### Completion experience and challenges

The initial setup took about one hour, which was longer than the listed 45 minutes. Creating the file and review layout required about 15 minutes. Reconstructing the dated items, counting the slips, and assigning a decision to each one required about 20 minutes. The evening entry and five calendar events used the remaining time.

After setup, the expected workload became a few minutes each evening and about 15 minutes every Sunday until the December 8 review. The most difficult part was counting uncomfortable slips without removing them or changing their dates. The memory-only rule also required accepting that the record described what I could recall, not a complete forensic history of every plan made during the week.

### Next learning goal

The first review felt more honest than expected because 5 slips among 9 dated items was uncomfortable to see. Giving each slip a new date, a drop, or a handover made the result actionable rather than leaving it as a discouraging number. The count showed where plans failed, while the evening entry showed which actions and conditions supported work that went well.

I am continuing the Sunday look-back and Evening Three until the December 8 review. At that point, I will follow the keep-or-stop conditions written before the first count. My next learning goal is to see whether repeated weekly records reveal plans that are routinely rescheduled, dropped, or handed over, while remembering that this personal practice describes patterns and does not establish their causes.
