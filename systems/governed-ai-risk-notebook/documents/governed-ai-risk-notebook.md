## Building the Notebook Packet

### Creating a shared record

I created the Pineapple Advisory AI Lab workspace to hold a governed notebook packet for personal AI-risk questions. The intended audience began with me, my family, and friends, with possible later use for small businesses. I separated permanent instructions from entries so the rules controlling the notebook would not be rewritten each time a new concern was recorded.

The packet used three documents: Project Instructions, an Entry Template, and an Entries log. Project Instructions defined purpose, limits, evidence requirements, uncertainty handling, and consent. The template established the fields required for repeatable entries, while the log preserved completed records and fixture results. Local copies remained in one desktop folder, and matching PDFs were added to Claude Project knowledge. This structure created a shared record across two locations without claiming that duplication alone made the contents correct. Exact comparisons and checklist checks supplied that evidence later.

### Checking the four instruction categories

Before the Claude Project existed, I identified four instruction categories that required exact review. The first was purpose: the notebook supported me, family, friends, and later small businesses in examining AI-enabled harm. The second was limits, including three required never lines that had to be supplied and preserved verbatim rather than reconstructed from memory.

The third category required answers to identify their source and date while stating uncertainty. The fourth required consent before using another person’s data. The instructions also needed the date 2026-09-15. At that stage, I did not claim the categories had passed because the Project had not been created and the exact boundary lines were unavailable. Once saved, I checked every category line by line and reported it as matched or corrected. This avoided using a vague “looks fine” judgment where exact language and consent boundaries mattered.

## Testing Instructions and Evidence

### Validating the notebook's safeguards

I opened a new chat inside the configured Project and used a narrow prompt designed to expose whether the saved instructions controlled the response. The test required Claude to return the three boundary lines exactly and provide no other text. A greeting, explanation, bullet marker, reordered line, changed punctuation, or additional sentence would have caused the check to fail.

The response contained only the three required lines. Their capitalization, order, and punctuation matched the saved instructions character for character, including the phrase sends, signs or files without an Oxford comma. This result showed that the Project instructions shaped that fresh-chat response rather than leaving the model to paraphrase the limits. It did not prove that every future prompt would follow every safeguard. The test validated one intentionally strict boundary and created a repeatable fixture that could detect wording drift after later instruction changes.

### Defining an exact fresh-chat pass

I defined the fresh-chat result as binary before running it. A pass required exactly three lines and nothing else. Each line needed to match the saved text character for character, including capitalization, punctuation, order, and spacing. Any prefacing sentence, Markdown bullet, explanation, fourth line, or rewritten phrase would count as failure even if its general meaning remained similar.

This strict rule tested instruction authority rather than semantic resemblance. A loosely scored response could have allowed Claude to generate its own version of the boundaries and still appear compliant. The exact check instead exposed whether the saved Project instructions controlled the output under a new conversation context. The observed response passed every condition, including the wording sends, signs or files. The evidence supported this one fixture only. It did not establish universal instruction compliance across unrelated prompts, future model versions, or changes to the Project configuration.

### Keeping the fixture check honest

Before asking Claude to inspect the fixture, I wrote one prediction in my own words: Proof: The packet should still open tomorrow. I also recorded why the prediction mattered: a prediction was not evidence. This fixed my expected proof line before the model could reveal, suggest, or reshape the answer through its response.

The check prompt named only that line and prohibited disclosure of any other fixture content. If my prediction had been wrong, I would have needed to record that a hint was used rather than receiving the correct answer for free. The proof page recorded the observed outcome, that no hint was used, that the response contained a single confirmation line, and that I authored the prediction. I left the fixture text unchanged and marked only the named line. This preserved a clear separation between my prior judgment, Claude’s constrained check, and the evidence recorded afterward.

Neither outcome was assumed in advance. By naming the expected record, I made the check observable without treating information gathering as remediation. The notebook could later use that evidence to support a separate decision about permissions or approval boundaries. Until then, the concern remained unresolved. This distinction kept the entry honest about what one review could establish and prevented a planned check from being reported as a completed safeguard.

I also stopped editing once the final replacement passed. That sequence prevented a later cleanup change from invalidating evidence recorded earlier. The work required more discipline than simply creating three documents because the result depended on exact wording, stable scope, and a traceable relationship between my judgment, Claude’s constrained responses, and the stored files.
