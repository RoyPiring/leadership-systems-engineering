## Choosing a Recurring Job to Estimate Better

### A planning-focused estimation goal

I built one Notion page to estimate recurring work from jobs I had already completed. The focus was my weekly operations and administration load, including status reports, invoice runs, server patching, code reviews, and backup checks. These jobs often ran beyond the time I expected, making the remembered estimates difficult to plan around.

The practice separated estimates supported by past work from estimates written as one unsupported number. I would later compare how often each group finished inside its estimate. The purpose was not to predict every job perfectly. It was to test whether looking backward at completed work produced a planning time that held up more often than relying on memory alone.

## Creating a Home for the Recording Practice

### Setting up one durable practice page

I created a blank Notion page titled Look Back Before You Put a Time on a Job. The page became the permanent home for the five-window plan, remembered table, weekly job lists, estimates, actual times, counting rules, gap, verdict, and cost note. Keeping these elements together made the evidence easier to follow over time.

I also opened Gmail and Google Calendar to confirm that the supporting tools were available under the same account. Calendar would hold only the Day 7, Day 28, and Day 66 checkpoints. It would not duplicate the jobs or estimation record. This gave the written practice one source of truth while Calendar supplied reliable return points for later checks.

### Keeping records in one place

All working content remained on the Notion page inside my Private section. The remembered times, recurring job types, ordered weekly lists, evidence trails, estimates, actuals, rules, delayed count, and cost note would be added to that same page. I did not create a second version in Gmail or Calendar.

Google Calendar contained only the three dated reminders needed to revisit the page. This separation prevented the practice from splitting into conflicting copies with different numbers or rules. Notion held the evolving record, while Calendar indicated when to inspect it. Anyone reviewing the page could therefore follow the evidence without searching across multiple tools for missing parts.

## Establishing the Remembered Baseline

### Building the remembered table and weekly list

I gave the Notion page its starting structure by adding the five-window plan and preserving every MAKES: line. I then created a remembered table with eight recurring job types. Entries included examples such as Server patching 1h and Code review 30 min, reflecting the times I would have used before checking completed work.

Below the table, I listed ten jobs planned for the current week. I left their estimates blank at this stage so the page showed the difference between naming work and defending a time for it. The remembered table represented the existing baseline, not verified duration evidence. The next stage would make the source of selected estimates visible before any actual time was recorded.

### Preserving first-thought job order

I wrote the ten weekly jobs in the order they came to mind and numbered them 1 through 10. I did not sort them by expected duration, urgency, difficulty, or how easy they would be to support with past evidence. The list began with the weekly status report and ended with checking backups before Friday.

Preserving that order determined which jobs entered the odd and even comparison without allowing me to place favorable tasks into either group later. I also left the estimates blank until the estimation method was applied. This protected the starting list from being reorganized around preferred results and showed that a time could not yet be defended merely because the job had appeared in the remembered table.

## Estimating New Jobs from Past Work

### Using completed work to support estimates

I added an estimate to each of the ten jobs. Every odd-numbered job received three parts: a Table says line, a sentence describing relevant completed work, and an ESTIMATE line. This made the difference between the remembered baseline and the revised planning time visible on the page.

Each even-numbered job received only one ESTIMATE line. I also pasted the ten-job worked example below the list so the expected format remained available during later weeks. This created two comparison groups without changing the original order. A reader could identify which estimates were supported by a past-work trail and which were plain estimates, then compare both groups with their recorded actuals.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/c8ddf0fa-0580-4a0e-8c6a-cba0f2c2b75e_4hf8njpr)

### Comparing odd-job evidence with even-job estimates

Job 3 demonstrated the evidence-supported format. Its first line read Table says: 1h. The next line stated that past vendor-update patch nights took 1h 30 when reboot checks were included. Its final line therefore read ESTIMATE: 1h 30, making the change from remembered time to supported estimate visible.

Job 4 contained only ESTIMATE: 30 min. It provided a usable planning number but no past-work trail explaining where that number came from. The difference between these entries was intentional. Later actuals would test whether the three-line odd-job method finished inside its estimate more often than the one-line even-job method, without claiming either estimate was correct in advance.

## Protecting the Practice with Rules and Reminders

### Fixing the rules before recording begins

I added a blank ACTUAL: field to all ten jobs before recording outcomes. I then copied the counting rules, research passage, limits, example count, cost rule, and verdict boundary onto the Notion page. These instructions established how started, completed, and unfinished work would be handled before any actual time could influence the method.

I created the Day 7, Day 28, and Day 66 reminders in Google Calendar after the rules were written. Freezing the method first prevented me from removing awkward jobs, moving the cutoff, or changing what counted after seeing which group performed better. Calendar supplied the review dates, while Notion retained the only copy of the rules and evidence used at those checkpoints.

### Deciding which jobs count

A job became countable after it received an actual time. Finishing exactly on the estimate counted as inside rather than over. A job that never started was excluded because it produced no timing evidence. A job that started but remained unfinished counted as over only when the time already spent exceeded its estimate.

I would not calculate the main result until the odd and even groups each contained their first 20 countable jobs. At that point, I would count the jobs completed inside their estimates for both groups and calculate one gap: odd inside minus even inside. This rule kept the comparison focused on whether visible past-work trails produced more inside-estimate results than plain estimates.

### Delaying the count until both groups are ready

Day 28 was a readiness check rather than a guaranteed calculation date. If either the odd or even group had fewer than 20 countable jobs, I would not calculate a partial gap. I would move the same check forward by one week and continue recording jobs under the existing rules.

The weekly delay would repeat until both groups held their first 20 countable results. This prevented an early imbalance from deciding the outcome when one group had more usable evidence than the other. The gap did not exist until both denominators were ready. Waiting protected the declared comparison without changing the required count, cutoff, job order, or definition of inside after actual times became visible.

## Rebuilding the Baseline with Checked Typical Times

### Verifying the middle actual before updating the table

I tested the future table-update method with three supplied Monthly report times: 2h 20, 2h 40, and 2h 30. I wrote the source times myself and sorted them from shortest to longest. Gemini returned a TYPICAL time of 2h 30, included the three source values, and did not calculate an average.

My ordered list was 2h 20, 2h 30, and 2h 40, so the returned typical value occupied the middle position. This confirmed the practice example without letting Gemini replace my own check. On Day 66, I will repeat the same process before changing any real row in the remembered table. A value must match the middle completed time before it becomes the new baseline.

## Reflecting on the Estimation Practice

### Tools and concepts applied

I used Notion as the single evidence page and Google Calendar for the Day 7, Day 28, and Day 66 reminders. The setup preserved a remembered baseline, retained the weekly jobs in first-thought order, and gave odd-numbered jobs visible past-work trails while even-numbered jobs received one estimate line.

I also defined what counted before recording began, delayed the gap until both groups reached 20 countable jobs, and checked a typical time by sorting three actuals instead of averaging them. The main lesson was that an estimate becomes easier to defend when its source remains visible. Writing the cutoff first also kept later results from changing the rule used to judge them.

### Time invested and challenges overcome

I completed the initial setup in just under one hour during one sitting. That work included creating the page, building the remembered table, listing the ten jobs, adding estimates and blank actual fields, copying the rules, and scheduling the three reminders. The full practice continues until Day 66 in mid-December.

The most demanding part was writing the odd-job evidence trails because each estimate needed a real completed job behind it. The page also exposed cases where patch nights and migration reviews had taken longer than the remembered table suggested. That difference was uncomfortable, but it showed why a visible past-work trail mattered more than defending the first number that came to mind.

### Skills developed and next learning goals

I learned to connect planning estimates to completed work rather than presenting each number without a source. The page now shows the remembered time, relevant past evidence, revised estimate, and later actual in one place. This structure will make misses inspectable instead of reducing them to a general feeling that recurring work ran long.

My next goal is to determine whether the odd-job evidence trails outperform the plain even-job estimates once both groups contain 20 countable jobs. The prewritten boundary asks whether the odd-inside count exceeds the even-inside count by 4 or more. I also want to rebuild real table rows from checked middle actuals on Day 66 without replacing them based on an average or one unusual job.
