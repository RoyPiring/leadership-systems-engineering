## Defining the Work Worth Protecting

### The work, Block, and people involved

I designed a proposed 90-minute morning Block for completing the monthly report on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Before creating a recurring calendar booking, I needed Sam and Priya to understand the schedule, know which urgent situations could interrupt it, and agree to protect the time. The agreement would be recorded in Notion before the Block appeared as a standing commitment.

The plan also defined how the practice would be judged. After agreement, I would classify eight Blocks as INTACT or BROKEN and compare the count with a fixed cutoff. This order mattered because a calendar event alone could not prove that the people affected by my availability had accepted the boundary. Agreement had to establish the shared rule before scheduling attempted to enforce it.

## Creating a Shared Home for Urgent Access

### Setting up the shared planning page

I created a Notion page titled Block Agreement: Monthly Report as the home for the proposal, urgent exceptions, interruption permissions, replies, habit rules, tally, cost questions, and later verdict. The page began with EXCEPTION LIST, followed by empty headings for every decision that would be added during the setup.

I also opened Gmail and Google Calendar under the same Google Account. Gmail remained available as a written communication route, while Calendar would hold only the review checkpoints until agreement was received. Giving each item one destination prevented the proposal, replies, rules, and dates from being scattered across unrelated tools or mistaken for an approved recurring booking.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/02b03c13-9af0-4483-8774-d461223e6bd2_5411wmcy)

### Putting urgent exceptions first

The page opened with the sentence, “Anything genuinely urgent is always taken, listed or not.” I placed it under EXCEPTION LIST, above WORK TO PROTECT and every later heading. This made the safety boundary visible before anyone encountered the request to protect the monthly report time.

Leading with the urgent rule prevented the Block from being read as a demand to ignore serious problems. Sam and Priya could see that the agreement governed normal interruptions while preserving access for real emergencies. This made the request easier to assess because the focus boundary and the emergency boundary appeared together rather than forcing others to guess which one took priority.

## Clarifying Who Can Interrupt the Block

### Matching people to allowed interruptions

I added two named interruption rows directly under the urgent rule: Sam | customer already on the phone and Priya | system has stopped. I then copied those same two rows under WHO INTERRUPTS. The wording remained identical in both places so the later proposal could not weaken or broaden either person’s access condition.

Each row connected one person with one specific event that could interrupt the monthly report Block. This made the agreement concrete enough to answer without turning it into a broad promise that every request would wait. It also reduced ambiguity during a live interruption because the page already showed who could enter and what condition allowed it.

### What each exception allows

Sam could interrupt the Block when a customer was already on the phone. Priya could interrupt when the system had stopped. Other requests from either person would wait until the 90-minute Block ended. These permissions defined normal urgent access without converting every incoming request into an exception.

The opening urgent rule still applied to a genuine emergency from anyone, even when that person or event did not appear in the two named rows. This created two levels of protection: specific expected interruptions for Sam and Priya, plus a broader emergency safeguard. The Block therefore protected ordinary focus time without claiming that the exception list could predict every serious event.

## Drafting a Block Before Agreement

### Creating an unagreed morning proposal

Under PROPOSAL, I recorded Tuesdays and Thursdays from 9:00 to 10:30, Work: Monthly report, The length is a guess, and Review: 23 October 2026. I then set the status to NOT AGREED - DO NOT BOOK. This identified the proposed schedule without presenting it as an accepted commitment.

The status separated planning from approval. A recurring event visible only on my calendar could imply that Sam and Priya had accepted reduced access when they had not. Keeping the Block off Calendar preserved an honest record of its state. The proposal could be discussed, revised, accepted, or rejected before the schedule began affecting the people expected to protect it.

### Treating the Block length as a guess

I wrote The length is a guess. immediately below Work: Monthly report in the proposal. This made uncertainty part of the agreement rather than hiding it behind a confident calendar duration.

The proposal called for reviewing the length after the first two Blocks. Sam, Priya, and I could then discuss whether the time was too short, too long, or workable based on experience. Naming the guess protected the initial schedule from becoming permanent simply because it was written first. The 90-minute duration was a testable starting point, not a settled fact.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/02b03c13-9af0-4483-8774-d461223e6bd2_ain6dmsn)

### Keeping the calendar clear until agreement

I checked Google Calendar for Tuesdays and Thursdays from 9:00 to 10:30 and confirmed that no recurring Monthly report Block existed. At the same time, the Notion page still displayed NOT AGREED - DO NOT BOOK. The two tools therefore showed the same state: a proposal had been written, but no approval had been assumed.

This check protected the meaning of a future calendar event. When the recurring Block eventually appeared, it should represent an agreement rather than a personal intention imposed on others. Booking it early would have communicated a boundary that Sam and Priya had never accepted. Keeping Calendar clear preserved the difference between proposing time and securing shared protection for it.

## Asking Others to Protect the Time

### Sending a proposal people can answer

I rewrote the PROPOSAL section as a complete question that Sam and Priya could answer. The request included the Tuesday and Thursday schedule, monthly report work, 90-minute guessed length, urgent exceptions, and planned review. I chose to present the proposal in person rather than send it by email.

After making the ask, I changed the page status to PROPOSAL SENT - AWAITING REPLIES. Google Calendar still contained no recurring monthly report Block. This preserved the agreement-before-booking rule while responses were pending. The schedule would remain a proposal until the people affected by it gave an answer that could be recorded under AGREEMENT STATUS.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/02b03c13-9af0-4483-8774-d461223e6bd2_bzorpjvo)

### Responding to no and partial agreement

If either person gave a first No, I would make one revised proposal rather than book the original schedule anyway. After a second No, I would record who declined and why, then stop without starting the eight-Block tally. This prevented repeated proposals from becoming pressure disguised as agreement seeking.

If only one person agreed, I would record only that name under AGREEMENT STATUS and treat the Block as agreed with that person alone. The recurring schedule would remain off Calendar until everyone affected by that booking had accepted it. Partial agreement could define a boundary with one person, but it could not be presented as shared protection from someone who had not said Yes.

## Establishing Fixed Rules and Review Dates

### Setting the habit, tally, cutoff, and dates

Under HABIT, I wrote the start-check-mark instruction and defined INTACT and BROKEN. I added eight blank tally lines and fixed the acceptance boundary at 6 intact Blocks out of 8. I also recorded the cost questions and nine limits before any Block occurred. These rules established how each session would be judged without knowing the outcome.

I scheduled the Block Review and Day 66 Verdict dates in Google Calendar after the rules were fixed. Choosing the definitions, count, and cutoff first prevented a difficult week from changing what qualified as success. It also kept each mark comparable because all eight Blocks would be classified against the same written conditions rather than explanations created after an interruption occurred.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/02b03c13-9af0-4483-8774-d461223e6bd2_c9mdd2qg)

### Using the review and Day 66 rules

The Block Review was scheduled for 23 October, and the Day 66 Verdict was scheduled for 1 December. During the early review, I would count the intact Blocks, discuss the schedule and guessed length with everyone involved, and complete the cost line. That checkpoint could inform a new agreement without rewriting the evidence already collected.

On Day 66, I would read both branches under KEEP OR DROP and apply them to the recorded count. Both dates pointed back to the same INTACT and BROKEN definitions and the fixed 6-out-of-8 cutoff established before Block one. The early review and final verdict had different purposes, but neither was allowed to redefine the original marks after seeing them.

## Pressure-Testing the Agreement Rules

### Classifying the hard cases

I tested the rules against difficult examples before beginning the tally. A listed customer call counted as INTACT because the agreement explicitly allowed it. Opening Gmail and stopping the report work counted as BROKEN. Moving the Block for a meeting also counted as BROKEN because the original protected time did not hold.

A real emergency would always be taken, but the Block would still be marked BROKEN. Partial agreement would operate only with the person who had said Yes, while a second No would result in NO COUNT and no tally. The emergency case required the most judgment because responding was correct even though the Block failed. The classification separated a good human decision from a successful focus session.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/pineapple_king_overclocked_the_ai/uploads/02b03c13-9af0-4483-8774-d461223e6bd2_qrpes6xt)

### Keeping the agreement and cutoff fixed

I added the edge cases and CHECKED list under AGREEMENT STATUS without changing the earlier sections. EXCEPTION LIST still began with the urgent rule, and the rows for Sam and Priya retained the same wording. The cutoff also remained 6 intact Blocks out of 8.

This placement preserved the original agreement while showing how its rules handled harder situations. Changing an exception or classification merely to make an awkward case appear INTACT would weaken the meaning of the later tally. Keeping the earlier text fixed meant all eight marks would refer to the same access rules, interruption definitions, and decision boundary written before the first Block.

## Reflecting on the Block Agreement Practice

### Tools and concepts learned

I used Notion for the agreement page and Google Calendar for the Block Review and Day 66 Verdict. Gmail remained available as a written communication route, although I chose to make the proposal in person. The recurring Tuesday and Thursday Block remained off Calendar while replies were pending.

The main concepts were agreement before booking, urgent exceptions first, person-specific access rules, fixed INTACT and BROKEN definitions, and a cutoff chosen before Block one. I also learned to distinguish doing the right thing during an emergency from preserving an intact Block. Protected time becomes shared only when the people affected by the boundary have knowingly accepted it.

### Project duration and challenges

I completed the setup in approximately 60 minutes, from creating the Notion page through classifying the final edge case. The most difficult part was scheduling the two review events because their instructions gave dates but no specific time or duration. I selected a short 3:00 PM slot for both checkpoints.

That choice matched the ten-minute note included in the Block Review details and gave each review a visible place on Calendar. The recurring monthly report Block was not booked because agreement was still pending. This distinction kept the scheduled review checkpoints from being mistaken for proof that Sam and Priya had accepted the proposed Tuesday and Thursday focus time.

### Next steps in building focus habits

I completed this work to learn how to protect focus time through agreement rather than by placing a private event on my calendar. The most reusable element was putting urgent exceptions first. That made the proposal safer and showed Sam and Priya that a protected Block would not remove access during a serious situation.

My next goal is to run a short review that changes the future plan when the evidence supports a change. After agreement and the first Blocks, I want to examine the intact count, costs, schedule, and guessed length without rewriting the original marks. Any revised duration or timing should become a new shared agreement rather than a silent calendar edit.
