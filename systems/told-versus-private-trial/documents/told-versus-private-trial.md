## Committing to a Private Accountability Practice

### Personal commitment and purpose

I ran a private comparison using six personal plans with fixed completion dates to see whether telling one person supported my follow-through. Date order assigned three plans to TOLD and three to PRIVATE, which prevented me from placing easier or more appealing actions in the group I wanted to perform better. The activity measured my own behavior and did not involve plans that other people expected me to complete.

Before recording any result, I wrote the rule that would determine whether I kept or stopped the practice. The final decision depended on the difference between the TOLD and PRIVATE completion counts, but only if my listener checked in on every TOLD date. Fixing this process first prevented me from changing the assignment method, completion standard, or decision rule after learning which plans I finished.

## Creating a Private Home for the Practice

### Setting up the practice tools

I prepared three apps before entering the six plans. I created a blank Notion page called Tell-or-Keep Practice under Private, then opened Google Calendar and Gmail using the same Google Account. Notion would hold the identity lines, assignments, results, calculations, and decision. Calendar would hold the dated reminders, while Gmail remained available as a backup method for telling my listener.

I tested the setup by saving one calendar event with a notification and one unsent message in Gmail. After confirming that both appeared in the correct places, I deleted the temporary event and draft. This left only the blank Notion page for the real record. Testing first reduced the risk of discovering a missing reminder or unavailable communication path after one of the six plans already depended on it.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/builtwithroy/uploads/1a459561-6fb4-45fe-bfe5-65135598088c_883j5s56)

### Verifying calendar and email access

The access test confirmed that Google Calendar could save a dated event with a reminder and that Gmail could retain an unsent message for my listener. The Tell-or-Keep access test event displayed a 30-minute notification, and the test message appeared in the Drafts folder without being sent. These checks verified the basic functions I planned to use before any real action or date entered the record.

The calendar would provide a prompt on each action date, while Gmail gave me a fallback if I could not tell my listener in person. Deleting both test items after verification kept them separate from the seven real events created later. This setup did not prove that I would complete the plans, but it established that the reminder and communication paths were available before the comparison began.

## Establishing Rules Before Results

### Fixing the practice rules

I entered the practice rules before writing any of the six plans. At the top of the Notion page, I added the telling rule and the boundary that kept identity lines private. I then added ten empty fields: Listener, Plan list, Identity line, TOLD or PRIVATE, Told, Mark, Listener asked, Difference, COST LINE, and Review date. I followed them with the review-date rule, keep-or-stop sentence, and research passage.

This order protected the record from later adjustment. No plan, assignment, completion mark, or listener response existed when the rules were written. The date-order method would control group placement, and the decision sentence would control the final action. Writing both in advance meant I could not move a difficult plan, change what counted as on time, or create a more favorable decision after seeing the results.

### Keeping the record ready for future results

The ten fields initially remained empty because the practice had not started. The page showed the full structure without a listener name, plan, identity statement, condition, outcome, count, cost note, or review result. Preserving that blank state demonstrated that the record and rules existed before I introduced information that could affect how the six plans were assigned or judged.

This mattered because the plans were not supposed to be divided according to difficulty, importance, or personal preference. If I had entered results while creating the structure, I could have chosen which actions to tell based on what I expected to finish. Keeping the fields blank made the sequence visible: define the rules, list all six plans, sort them by date, assign the conditions, and only then begin recording outcomes.

## Assigning Plans by Date and Telling a Listener

### Listing plans before date-order assignment

I selected Sam as my listener and wrote six personal plans with private identity lines. After all six existed, I sorted them by date and used their positions to assign the conditions. Plans in positions 1, 3, and 5 became TOLD, while positions 2, 4, and 6 became PRIVATE. I told Sam the three assigned actions and their dates in person rather than selecting the plans I preferred to discuss.

I also created seven Google Calendar events: one for each of the six action dates and one for the later review. This ensured that every plan had a dated reminder under the same account used during setup. Listing, sorting, assigning, telling, and scheduling in that order preserved the intended comparison. My preference did not determine which actions received listener awareness, and every plan received the same calendar support.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/builtwithroy/uploads/1a459561-6fb4-45fe-bfe5-65135598088c_q7o1vymd)

### Protecting identity lines while assigning conditions

After sorting, fixing the door hinge, cooking a new recipe, and finishing my library book occupied positions 1, 3, and 5, so they became TOLD. The garage, photos, and bulbs appeared in positions 2, 4, and 6, so they became PRIVATE. The alternating date-order rule created two groups of three without using the meaning or expected difficulty of each action as the assignment method.

When I spoke with Sam, I shared only each TOLD action and its date. I did not share any identity line from the Notion page. Those lines described private personal meaning and were not required for Sam to understand the plans or check in later. Keeping them local limited the disclosure to information needed for the practice while preserving the complete personal record in one private location.

## Recording Each Dated Outcome

### Making an on-time record for every plan

On each plan date, I checked whether the action had been completed by that date and entered one mark. Plans 1, 2, and 3 received Done on time. Plans 4, 5, and 6 received Not done on time. I finished the library book one day late, but it remained Not done on time because the rule measured completion by the original date rather than eventual completion.

I made each entry when the evening calendar reminder appeared and did not move any date afterward. This prevented a late result from becoming successful through rescheduling or memory. The mark described only whether the action met its stated date, not how difficult it was or whether it was completed later. Applying the same boundary to all six plans kept the TOLD and PRIVATE working counts comparable.

![Image](https://nextwork.ai/builtwithroy/uploads/1a459561-6fb4-45fe-bfe5-65135598088c_cdhkwaxq)

### Tracking listener follow-through on TOLD dates

Each TOLD plan included a Listener asked field that recorded whether Sam checked in on its scheduled date. Sam asked about the door hinge on October 21, the new recipe on October 28, and the library book on November 4. I therefore entered Listener asked: Yes for all three TOLD actions. The PRIVATE plans did not receive this field because Sam had not been told about them.

This record mattered because the prewritten decision rule required listener follow-through before the difference could guide my choice. Merely telling Sam once would not establish that the intended accountability condition occurred on the three relevant dates. Recording each check-in separately showed that the TOLD condition included both disclosure and dated follow-up, allowing the comparison to proceed under the rule written before the plans began.

## Comparing TOLD and PRIVATE Results

### Turning date marks into a comparison

I counted the on-time marks within each condition after all six dates passed. TOLD plans 1 and 3 were completed on time, producing a TOLD count of 2. PRIVATE plan 2 was completed on time, producing a PRIVATE count of 1. I then recorded Sam’s listener result as Yes and completed the COST LINE using words instead of introducing another rating, score, or performance measure.

The two counts served as inputs for one outcome: the difference between TOLD and PRIVATE. Keeping the listener answer and cost note separate prevented them from becoming competing metrics. The calculation described what happened across these six personal plans, while the written cost captured how the practice felt or what it required. Neither part was treated as evidence about how other people would respond.

### Calculating the difference

I followed the prewritten formula by subtracting the PRIVATE count from the TOLD count. Two TOLD plans and one PRIVATE plan were completed on time, so the calculation was 2 - 1 = 1. I entered Difference: 2 minus 1 is 1 directly beneath the working counts. Because the result was above zero, TOLD performed better within this six-plan comparison.

The positive difference did not establish that telling someone causes better completion. The sample contained only my six plans, and date order created the groups without balancing their difficulty or required effort. The result only supported a personal preference under the conditions I recorded. Keeping that boundary visible prevented a one-person comparison from becoming a general claim about accountability practices.

### Understanding the working counts

The TOLD count of 2 and PRIVATE count of 1 were working values used to calculate the difference. I placed them immediately above the Difference line so the arithmetic could be inspected without searching through all six entries. I did not treat either count as a separate final result because doing so would create multiple numbers that could be interpreted selectively.

The Difference line remained the only outcome number. This kept the review tied to the exact comparison defined before the plans began and prevented me from emphasizing whichever group count supported the conclusion I preferred. The working values still provided transparency, but the prewritten rule evaluated their difference only after Sam’s three dated check-ins had been confirmed.

## Following the Decision Written in Advance

### Using the listener result to follow the prewritten action

On the review date, I first checked whether Sam had asked about every TOLD plan on its scheduled date. All three Listener asked fields were Yes, so the difference was permitted to guide the decision. The recorded difference was 1, which was above zero and matched the TOLD is better branch of the keep-or-stop sentence written before any plan date passed.

I recorded that I would keep telling one person the action and date for future plans. I described this as my preference based on these six plans, not as proof that the method causes better follow-through. The decision followed the original rule without changing a date, completion mark, listener response, count, or threshold. This preserved the full path from advance commitment to final action.

## Reflecting on the Practice

### Tools and concepts learned

I used Notion for the private record, Google Calendar for the six action reminders and review event, and Gmail as a backup communication path. Notion kept the identity lines away from the listener while holding the rules, assignments, results, calculation, cost note, and final decision. Calendar ensured that every action was checked on its stated date instead of reconstructed later.

The main concepts were prewriting the rules, using date order for assignment, keeping identity lines private, and marking each plan only on its scheduled date. I also learned to treat the TOLD and PRIVATE counts as calculation inputs rather than separate conclusions. Calling the outcome a preference instead of proof kept the interpretation consistent with a six-plan personal comparison.

### Completion time and challenges

The first session took approximately 75 minutes. That time covered building the Notion page, writing six plans and their identity lines, assigning the conditions, telling Sam, and creating seven calendar events. Each plan date required about one minute to check and mark. Counting the results and writing the comparison took around 20 minutes, followed by about 10 minutes for the review-date decision.

The hardest part was identifying six personal plans that nobody else already expected me to complete. Plans involving another person’s needs, a work deadline, or a shared obligation would not have been private commitments because outside expectations could affect completion. Restricting the list to self-directed actions kept the comparison closer to its stated purpose, even though the six plans still differed in effort and difficulty.

### Personal learning reflection

I completed this practice to examine whether telling one person about a dated action supported my own follow-through. Using six real plans, one listener, fixed dates, and an advance decision rule gave me a record I could inspect rather than relying on a general impression. Sam’s three dated check-ins confirmed that the TOLD condition occurred, and the final difference of 1 supported keeping the practice as a personal preference.

My next goal is to plan the week so important actions receive protected time before other commitments fill the calendar. The tell-or-keep comparison addressed disclosure and follow-up, but it did not test whether each action had enough time reserved for completion. Adding protected work blocks would examine a different part of the planning process without changing what this six-plan record showed.
