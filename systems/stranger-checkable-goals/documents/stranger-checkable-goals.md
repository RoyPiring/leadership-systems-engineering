## Setting a Clear Purpose for Checkable Goals

### The purpose behind the goals page

I built this goals page to replace vague intentions with targets that could be checked from visible evidence. Each retained goal needed to identify the accountable person, state an exact deadline, and define a result that someone else could count. This made completion depend on recorded facts rather than my interpretation of whether I had made enough progress.

The system also separated goal writing from later judgment. I preserved the original list, measured its starting quality, rewrote selected goals, marked one genuine stretch target, and established review rules before tracking began. This sequence created a record of what changed and prevented the final evaluation from relying on memory. The page did not guarantee that I would complete every goal. It created a consistent way to determine whether I followed through, missed the target, needed to revise it, or should drop it after the review period.

## Preserving an Honest Starting Baseline

### Creating the workspace before evaluating goals

I created the workspace and entered my original nine goals before applying any scoring rules. This preserved how I naturally described the outcomes when I was not trying to satisfy a checklist. I then prepared the evaluation fields used to test whether each sentence named a specific outcome, included a measurable number, and stated a deadline.

Keeping setup separate from evaluation protected the baseline from accidental correction. If I had reviewed the criteria before entering the goals, I could have added dates and numbers while believing I was only copying my original thoughts. The untouched list gave the later comparison a fixed starting point. It also kept every original sentence in the denominator, including goals I might later rewrite or remove. The baseline therefore measured the quality of the full starting list rather than only the goals that were easiest to convert into checkable targets.

### Why the original list remained untouched

I left the original list unchanged because it represented how I wrote goals before learning the checkability rules. Showing the criteria first would have influenced the wording and created a stronger-looking baseline. That would have made the later before-and-after comparison unreliable because both versions would already reflect the same method.

The preserved list also provided evidence of what the rewriting step accomplished. I could compare each original sentence with its revised form and identify the added owner, deadline, and countable result. Without that record, the rewritten goals might look clear, but I could not show whether the system changed my goal-writing or merely replaced text that was already specific. The untouched baseline was not intended to embarrass or score my intentions. It created a fair control condition so I could measure the method against my natural starting point and keep hindsight from changing the evidence.

### Freezing and measuring the original list

I froze the nine sentences under STARTING LIST - DO NOT EDIT before scoring them. I treated each sentence as one goal and evaluated it against the same three conditions: a specific action or outcome, a measurable number, and a deadline. A goal counted as checkable only when it satisfied all three requirements in its original wording.

This method prevented partial credit from inflating the result. A number without a due date could show scale but could not establish lateness. A clear activity without a count could not show how much completion was required. I also kept goals in the denominator even if I later decided not to retain them. Removing weak entries before scoring would have made the baseline appear stronger than the list I actually wrote. The frozen measurement produced a reproducible starting point that could be checked by another reader using the same nine sentences and criteria.

### Calculating the baseline fraction

The denominator was nine because the starting block contained nine goal sentences, and I counted every one. The numerator was zero because no sentence passed all three checks. Four goals included a number, five described a specific action or outcome, and none contained a deadline. Since every goal was missing at least one required field, the first count was 0 out of 9.

I reported the component counts so the baseline explained more than the final fraction. The result did not mean that all nine goals were meaningless. Several already contained useful specificity or measurable quantities, but none gave a stranger enough information to decide whether the goal had been completed on time. The zero established the exact weakness that rewriting needed to address. It also prevented later progress from being described vaguely. Any revised goal that contained a named owner, countable result, and exact date would represent a documented change from the original list.

## Building a Stranger-Checkable Goals System

### Rewriting goals for visible accountability

I selected three to five retained goals and rewrote each one as a short sentence containing an owner, deadline, and countable result. The wording needed to let a stranger decide whether the goal was complete without asking what I meant by terms such as “more,” “better,” or “consistent.” I kept each sentence focused on one observable outcome.

The revised goals also needed evidence that could be recorded during normal execution. Counts such as sessions completed, nights followed, or deliverables published created direct review points. Exact dates established when the count became due, while naming Owner: Roy Piring Jr. removed ambiguity about accountability. This format did not measure the personal value of a goal or guarantee that the target was appropriately difficult. It made the result testable. Difficulty was evaluated separately through the HARD marker, evidence-based objections, and the later keep-or-drop review.

### The owner, date, and count rule

The rule required three elements inside every rewritten sentence. The named owner identified who was accountable, such as Owner: Roy Piring Jr., instead of forcing a reviewer to infer responsibility from the page. The deadline used an exact date, such as November 20, 2026, so the reviewer could determine whether the target was completed late or on time.

The countable result defined how much evidence completion required. Numbers such as 45 sessions or six nights could be tallied, while terms such as “exercise more” or “sleep better” could not. All three elements had to appear together because each solved a different ambiguity. The owner answered who, the date answered when, and the count answered how much. A goal missing one element could still express a useful intention, but it did not pass the stranger-checkable standard used by this system.

## Precommitting to a Hard Goal and Clear Decision Rules

### Making the stretch goal and rules visible

I marked one retained goal with HARD to identify it as the stretch target before the tracking period began. I then added a seven-line tracking block beneath the goals. It recorded the baseline count, reading trigger, review period, expected personal cost, and the evidence needed for the final decision. Keeping these details visible made the challenge and evaluation method part of the page rather than private assumptions.

I also wrote the keep-and-drop rules before recording any review results. The decision used a strict four-of-five-day threshold and included a reminder condition that could only push the outcome toward Drop. This prevented me from weakening the rule after seeing an uncomfortable score. The precommitment did not guarantee honest tracking, but it removed several opportunities for convenient reinterpretation. The final decision had to follow the written boundary or explicitly document why the rule was being changed.

### Using one threshold for every outcome

I used one four-of-five-day boundary because it assigned every possible score to exactly one outcome. Scores of four or five satisfied the keep condition, while scores from zero through three did not. This avoided an undecided middle range where the same result could be defended as either success or failure after the review.

Fixing the boundary in advance also limited hindsight bias. If I recorded three successful days, I could not lower the threshold to three and claim that the habit had passed. If I recorded four, I could not raise the standard because the result felt too easy. The reminder clause strengthened the same decision rather than introducing a second numerical threshold. If the habit depended on someone or something reminding me, that evidence pushed toward Drop. The rule therefore measured whether the selected routine supported independent action, not whether reminders could temporarily produce compliance.

### Anchoring the reading habit and review

I selected one genuine stretch goal in Notion and marked it HARD so its intended difficulty remained visible throughout the review. I placed a seven-line tracking block beneath the goals to preserve the baseline count, reading trigger, review dates, personal cost, completion evidence, and decision rule in one location.

I scheduled the 66-day review before tracking began and connected the reading action to an existing daily routine. This reduced the need to make a fresh decision about when to read the goals. The page also recorded what the habit would cost me, which prevented an ambitious target from being presented as effortless. I precommitted the keep-and-drop standard before any results existed so the final judgment would follow a declared rule. The system could still reveal that the goal or trigger was poorly chosen, but that finding would appear through recorded evidence rather than an informal change in expectations.

### The existing routine that triggers reading

I selected breaking my fast with the first meal at 12:00 PM as the trigger for reading the goals. The event appeared on my calendar every day, continued indefinitely, and involved a physical action I would notice without a separate alert. That made it a stronger anchor than a task that existed only because a reminder appeared.

I rejected work start, treadmill use, and Chirp as the primary trigger because each depended on notifications or routines that were not equally stable. Using them could make the review measure reminder delivery rather than whether I remembered to read the goals from an existing habit. The noon meal was not proof that I would follow through, and the calendar did not contain compliance data. It provided a repeatable cue that met the “nobody reminding me” condition in the keep rule. The 66-day tracking period would determine whether the cue worked in practice.

## Testing Whether the Goals Are Truly Challenging

### Deciding which too-easy objections remained valid

I tested each “too easy” objection against evidence already available in the calendar, reminder settings, creation dates, and logged results. An objection survived when the records supported it or when I lacked enough evidence to refute it. I did not dismiss an objection only because I felt confident that the goal would be difficult.

The eating-window objection remained valid because the calendar showed three months of scheduled events but contained no compliance record. That evidence could not disprove the claim that the routine was already normal. The strength-program objection failed because the program had been created only six days earlier and contained no logged history. There was no evidence that “just showing up” had become an established behavior, so the HARD marker remained justified. This method separated documented difficulty from optimism and kept challenge judgments tied to facts another reviewer could inspect.

## Reflecting on the Goal-Setting Practice

### Tools and concepts applied

I used Notion to preserve the starting list, store rewritten goals, mark the stretch target, and maintain the tracking block. Google Calendar supplied dated routines and scheduled the 66-day review, while Gmail supported the notification context examined during the reminder test. Each tool contributed evidence but did not independently determine whether a goal passed.

The central method was to establish an untouched baseline before editing, then require every retained goal to name an owner, deadline, and countable result. I also used precommitment to set the review boundary before results existed and red-teaming to challenge whether the HARD goal was genuinely difficult. Separating checkability from difficulty mattered: a goal could be measurable yet too easy, or ambitious yet impossible to score. The completed system tested both qualities through different evidence and rules.

### Time invested and key challenge

I completed the build in approximately 60 minutes. The hardest part was red-teaming the goals without turning the exercise into personal opinion. I needed objections that could be tested against calendar history, reminder configuration, creation dates, or logged behavior. A statement such as “this feels easy” did not provide enough evidence to retain or remove the HARD marker.

The process also required discipline around the untouched baseline. Once I understood the owner, date, and count rule, it was tempting to repair the original goals and raise the starting score. Preserving all nine sentences kept the 0 out of 9 result honest and made the rewrite measurable. These constraints made the final page more than a polished list. It became a record of the original wording, the applied rules, the declared stretch target, and the evidence required for the 66-day decision.

### What this project enabled next

I completed this build to turn abstract intentions into checkable goals supported by a 66-day review plan. The system preserved a 0 out of 9 baseline, rewrote selected goals with named owners, exact deadlines, and countable outcomes, and marked one target as HARD. It also established a routine-based reading trigger and a four-of-five-day decision boundary before tracking began.

My next step is to run the review without changing the threshold or rewriting the original evidence. I want to record whether the noon meal reliably triggers goal reading, whether reminders are required, and whether the stretch target remains difficult once execution begins. I also plan to apply the same red-team method to larger personal initiatives. The goal is not to make every plan harder. It is to ensure that ambition, completion, and revision decisions can be supported by records instead of confidence or memory.
